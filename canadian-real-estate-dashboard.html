The rate story just flipped

Pins show current position; grey marks show three months ago

For two years every forecast assumed the next move in rates was down. That assumption broke this autumn. Inflation reached 3%, bond yields pushed fixed mortgage rates up across all six big banks, and several major forecasters now expect the Bank of Canada to raise its policy rate on 28 October. Housing has gone quiet in response — sales and prices have been close to flat for four months. Commercial has gone the other way: office and industrial both tightened sharply in the third quarter, because almost nothing is being built.

What changed since September

Four things. Inflation hit 3% and the rate debate inverted — UBS, Manulife, Oxford Economics and Scotiabank have all moved to calling an October hike, while RBC calls it a close call. Fixed mortgage rates rose 10 to 50 basis points across the big six since early September. Office vacancy fell again to 16.7% with the largest quarterly drop in sublease space since 2005. And industrial absorption of 10.3 million sq. ft. was the strongest quarter since 2022, ending any question about whether that market had bottomed.

Residential

CREA, August 2026 package released 15 September; September data due 16 October

August broke the summer's run of gains: sales slipped 0.7% while new listings rose 3.3%, ending three months of declining supply. The sales-to-new-listings ratio fell to 49.1%, the loosest reading of the year. Prices did not move — the HPI was flat month over month and is down 3.0% on the year. CREA's own read is that the economy, not the housing market, is now the problem: rising inflation risk, higher fixed mortgage rates, and doubts about the durability of growth are expected to weigh on momentum into 2027.

The long view matters for reading today's numbers. The average price peaked at $706,466 in 2022 and has stayed within roughly 4% of that level every year since, so the post-2022 story is stagnation rather than a crash. Volumes are the softer half of the picture: five years after the 2021 peak, transaction counts are still running roughly 30% below it, and CREA's forecast does not bring them back within the decade's range before 2028.

CMHC's deputy chief economist expects the downward trend to continue, with construction moderating further toward year-end. The regional split has shifted since the summer: Toronto's six-month trend is now flat, while Vancouver and Montreal are both up 6% on multi-unit starts. The 142,423 permitted-but-unstarted units keep growing — projects that clear approval but not the financing test.

The condominium problem, and its mirror image

A floor, finally

First sales gain since 2023

GTHA new condo sales rose 52% year over year in the second quarter to 702 units — the first annual increase since Q3 2023. Sales of completed, developer-held units more than tripled to 535, helped by bulk deals with investment groups. The level is still 86% below the ten-year average for a second quarter.

Still stuck

$1,186 new versus $830 resale

Developer-held completed inventory hit a record 5,001 units, up 68% on the year. Asking prices on that standing stock averaged $1,186 per square foot — a 43% premium over recently registered resale product at $830. Pre-construction sales fell 80% to just 50 units.

The hole ahead

Pipeline down 62% from peak

Combined pre-construction and under-construction inventory fell 37% year over year to 48,710 units, against a 2022 peak near 127,000. Construction starts totalled 448 units, no new projects launched for a second straight quarter, and 11,653 units have been cancelled since the start of 2024.

Ontario's enhanced HST rebate — up to $130,000 on new homes under $1 million, with royal assent on 18 June 2026 — is what moved completed inventory. It is also why pre-construction collapsed: to qualify, construction must start before 31 March 2027 and be substantially complete by 31 December 2029, which almost no new launch can meet. The policy cleared the shelf and emptied the pipeline at the same time.

Rental

Rentals.ca and Urbanation, September 2026; vacancy from CMHC's annual survey

September marked two full years of falling asking rents, the longest run in the report's recent history. At $2,034 the national average is 7.3% below two years ago and 9.2% off the May 2024 peak of $2,202. But the monthly decline was a single dollar, and Toronto and Vancouver — which lead the national series — have been trending higher for six months after bottoming at five-year lows.

Commercial

CBRE Q3 2026 office and industrial figures; retail from the H1 2026 rent survey

Commercial is now the strong half of Canadian real estate, and the reason is arithmetic rather than exuberance. Office construction sits near a 22-year low with no significant completions expected until 2032. Industrial deliveries this year will be the lowest in eight. Against a frozen pipeline, ordinary demand is enough to tighten everything at once.

Nine of eleven markets posted positive absorption, with downtown vacancy falling in eight — Toronto led at 90 basis points. Vancouver was the outlier, pushed up by a single tech tenant consolidating. Sublease space fell 1.3 million sq. ft., the largest quarterly drop since 2005. The pipeline ticked up for the first time in four years but is only 23.2% pre-leased, down from over 60% a year ago.

The gap inside the office market, Q3 2026

Best-in-class space is effectively full while older stock is not recovering. Trophy vacancy is 6.3% nationally — 2.7% in Toronto and 3.0% in Montreal — against 23.8% for downtown Class B and C. CBRE's read is that B and C vacancy is improving mainly because obsolete buildings are being removed, not because they are leasing.

Industrial, retail and the rest

Industrial

The turn arrived in force

Availability fell 30 basis points to 5.2%, the largest quarterly decline in five years, with eight of eleven markets tightening. Net absorption of 10.3 million sq. ft. was the strongest quarter since 2022 and 3.7 times the trailing three-year average, bringing the year to 18.8 million. Calgary alone took 3.8 million sq. ft., its second-best quarter on record; Edmonton was the only negative market.

Rents are still falling but more slowly: $14.77 per sq. ft., down 2.4% against Q2's 3.8%. Deliveries this year will total 19.3 million sq. ft., the lowest in eight years.

Retail

Tight, and quietly dependable

Vacancy stayed tight through the first half, especially in grocery-anchored suburban centres, with available space effectively at capacity in several high-growth corridors. Demand keeps shifting toward services — medical, fitness, personal care and quick-service food — while experiential tenants backfill large-format boxes.

New supply is the constraint: retail delivery is tied to mixed-use development, which has slowed to a crawl.

Multifamily and seniors

One softening, one squeezed

Multifamily is in a transition year as softer rental demand and continued deliveries lift vacancy — though it was also the largest single destination for investment capital in the first half, at $6.9 billion.

Seniors housing is the opposite trade: an ageing population is driving double-digit rent growth while construction stays limited, because market rents still sit below what new builds need to pencil. Competition for those assets is intensifying.

Capital markets

Volumes from Altus Group, H1 2026; cap rates from CBRE's Q2 2026 survey

Transaction volume is running well ahead of last year. First-half investment reached $24.1 billion, up 19%, excluding the Alberta markets where data was incomplete. That puts the year on track for CBRE's roughly $56 billion forecast, which would be the third-highest total on record. Cap rates appear to have peaked: the national all-property average compressed to 6.58% in the second quarter, with a 320 basis point spread over the Canada ten-year.

What the money is chasing

Office, still the surprise. Office investment rose 61% in the first half. The bet is supply arithmetic: with 1.4 million sq. ft. under construction and nothing significant delivering before 2032, today's best buildings have no future competition.

Multifamily, at scale. At $6.9 billion and up 67%, apartments were the single largest sector. Softer rents have not deterred buyers underwriting a supply cliff three years out.

Data centres. Microsoft has committed roughly $7.5 billion to Canadian cloud and AI infrastructure with its first new facility due in the second half of this year. Around Toronto, a 112 MW project on Langstaff Road anchors a corridor of roughly 360 MW planned long term.

Distressed condo inventory. Bulk buyers tripled their take of completed developer-held units, converting ownership stock to rental.

Cap rate figures are the most recent published, covering the second quarter. CBRE's Q3 cap rate survey is expected later this month, and the sharp move in bond yields since September makes it the number to watch.

Geography

Prices and forecasts from CREA; starts from CMHC, January to August 2026

The decade-long pattern has inverted. British Columbia and Ontario — the two provinces that drove national price growth through the 2010s — are the only two CREA forecasts to see prices fall this year. Atlantic Canada, Quebec and the Prairies have now carried national price growth for three consecutive years. Construction tells a different regional story again: Alberta has given back 16% of its starts while Manitoba is up 21%.

Policy and innovation

Federal measures through the Spring Economic Update 2026 and Royal Assent, June 2026

The most consequential change in Canadian housing is institutional rather than technological. Ottawa has stopped subsidising demand and started acting as a buyer, on the theory that a factory-built housing industry needs guaranteed customers more than it needs grants. Whether that scales is still an open question — the Parliamentary Budget Officer puts Build Canada Homes at about 26,000 units over five years, roughly 3.7% of what it estimates is needed to restore affordability.

Build Canada Homes

A Crown corporation with a purchase order

Launched September 2025 with $13 billion in initial capitalisation; the enabling Act received Royal Assent in June 2026. Alongside it sit $1 billion in equity and $25 billion in debt financing aimed at Canadian prefabricated builders.

Six federal Direct Build sites — Toronto, Winnipeg, Edmonton, Ottawa, Dartmouth and Longueuil — break ground this half for up to about 4,000 homes, with procurement explicitly favouring modular and factory-built construction.

The volume is small and the PBO's projection makes that plain. The mechanism is the point: modular factories fail on throughput rather than technology, and a forward order book measured in years is the one thing the private market has never produced on its own.

Spending and rules

What changed for buyers and builders

$6 billion to recruit, train and hire skilled trades workers nationally, plus $41.9 million over five years from 2026–27 to streamline National Model Codes and speed approvals for modular and factory-built housing.

More than $7 billion in low-cost lending accelerated through the Apartment Construction Loan Program, supporting up to 16,500 rental homes in British Columbia alone.

In Ontario, an enhanced rebate of up to $130,000 on new homes under $1 million, in force since June. Its 2027 and 2029 construction deadlines are now shaping which projects move.

Unchanged: the stress test at contract rate plus two points, the $1.5 million insured mortgage cap, and 30-year amortisations for first-time buyers and new builds.

Technology, with the hype removed

Proptech funding reset

Canadian proptech companies raised about C$450 million in 2025, down from roughly C$800 million in 2024, with fewer new startups. Toronto holds around 45% of the country's proptech firms. The constraint has moved from innovation to execution at scale, and companies are prioritising revenue milestones over the next round.

Robots on the framing line

The clearest signal of maturity is partnership rather than disruption: Mattamy Homes, one of Canada's largest builders, is scaling automated homebuilding with Promise Robotics. Pairing emerging technology with real operating environments is what closes the gap between pilot and production.

AI expectations, recalibrated

The share of commercial real estate executives reporting transformative impact from AI fell to roughly 1%, from about 12% a year earlier. Where returns do show up — energy optimisation, lease abstraction, acquisition screening — they follow from fixing data foundations first. Pilots are easy; production is not.

Five structural shifts worth tracking

Office inventory is being permanently reduced

Conversions have removed 9.6 million sq. ft. since 2021 and demolitions a further 3.0 million, cutting national stock by 2.7%. Vacancy is falling partly because the denominator is shrinking — and CBRE now expects no significant new completions before 2032.

Rental is absorbing the condo market's failure

Cancelled presale projects are converting to purpose-built rental, and bulk buyers are moving completed ownership stock into the rental pool. That softens rents now and deepens the ownership-supply hole around 2028–29.

AI infrastructure is a new property type here

Power availability, not land, is becoming the binding constraint on industrial-adjacent development in the Toronto and Montreal corridors.

Demographics are reshaping the bid

Seniors housing has the strongest fundamentals of any asset class and the least new supply, because construction costs sit above what current rents justify.

Regional convergence is under way

Saskatchewan and Newfoundland lead price growth this year while British Columbia and Ontario decline. Affordability, not amenity, is now the strongest predictor of where prices rise, and three decades of interprovincial divergence are starting to unwind.

Affordability tool

Illustrative only — not a mortgage quote

Payments use Canadian semi-annual compounding. The qualifying figure applies the federal stress test, which requires borrowers to qualify at the higher of their contract rate plus two percentage points or 5.25%. Income required assumes a 32% gross debt service ratio including property tax and heat. The default rate is the roughly 4.34% available on insured five-year fixed mortgages through brokers in early October — bank posted specials run higher, around 4.79% to 5.14%.

UBS, Manulife, Oxford Economics and Scotiabank have all moved to calling an increase on 28 October; UBS and Manulife see a second by January. RBC holds near term and calls the decision a close call. Two quarter-point moves would put the policy rate at 2.75%.

Inflation

CPI at 3% and expected higher

August inflation reached the top of the Bank's control range, driven by energy. UBS projects 3.3% when September data lands on 19 October. That print is the single biggest input into the rate decision nine days later.

Credit
Elevated

Fixed mortgage rates are rising again

All six big banks raised select fixed rates between early September and early October, by 10 to 50 basis points. Borrowers renewing into this now face a higher floor than they did three months ago, and the stress test compounds it.

Trade
Moderate

Tariffs and CUSMA

US tariffs and Canadian counter-measures remain in place. Industrial absorption has been strong regardless, but construction input costs stay directly exposed.

Supply
Watch

A construction air pocket around 2028

Weak starts today become weak completions in three years, just as the current pipeline empties. In the GTHA condo market the cliff is already visible: inventory down 62% from its 2022 peak. Rental relief now is borrowed from later.
